 PART 1 - WHAT IT DOES
================================================================================

THE PROBLEM
-----------
Walking a field to find weeds, dry spots or struggling crops takes hours, and
problems are easy to miss until they spread. Professional mapping drones and
agronomy software often cost thousands of dollars. Many small farms can't
justify that.

THE IDEA
--------
This project turns a low-cost consumer drone, the Potensic Atom 2, into a field
scouting tool. Software that runs on an ordinary laptop does the analysis.

WHAT YOU CAN DO WITH IT
-----------------------
* Describe your field with four what3words addresses
  Every 3 m x 3 m square on Earth has a unique three-word name, such as
  ///filled.count.soap. Enter the four corners of your field and the app draws
  it on a satellite map and works out its size. No surveying, no drawing by
  hand.

* Plan the flight automatically
  The app plans a back-and-forth route over the whole field, with photos
  spaced so they overlap. It estimates flight time and splits long flights
  into battery-sized parts. Missions are built for the drone's own app,
  Potensic Eve.

* See your whole field from above
  After the flight, import the photos straight from the drone's memory card.
  The app matches the overlapping photos in 3D (structure from motion) and
  joins them into one seamless, true-to-scale map of the field, then checks
  that map for crop health and weeds. Without the 3D-matching software it
  places each photo by its GPS position instead.

* Crop-health maps
  The field is coloured from red (stressed or bare) to green (healthy), using
  four crop-health measures that work from ordinary colour photos. The app
  marks the patches that need attention with numbered pins.

* Weed maps
  A model trained on expert-labelled drone photos of a real cotton field
  outlines likely weed patches in purple and reports how much of the field
  they cover.

* One number for crop health, one for weeds
  After the analysis, Eagle Eye sums up the whole field in two numbers:
  a crop-health score out of 100 (for example 96 = Good) and the share of the
  field with weeds (for example 4.4% = Moderate). One click shows on the map
  exactly which spots need a look and why: thin crop, pale crop or bare
  patches.

* Send the drone back for a closer look
  One click plans a low flight over each problem patch, for close-up photos
  that the weed check analyses again.

* Fly to one spot
  Type one what3words address and the drone goes to that square, takes a
  photo, and the app checks that spot for crop health and weeds.

* Works on the phone too
  A simple web page turns what3words addresses into the exact coordinates to
  enter in the drone app on iPhone, with a map of numbered pins.

WHY IT MATTERS
--------------
* Low cost: a consumer drone plus free, open-source software.
* Saves time: scout a whole field in one short flight instead of walking it.
* Targeted action: treat or check only the patches that need it. That saves
  herbicide, fertiliser and labour.
* Easy to share: a what3words address is easy to say, text or write down when
  telling a worker or advisor exactly where the problem is.
* Grounded in research: the methods follow published drone and satellite
  farming research. The project README reviews 15 papers.

TESTED ON A REAL FARM
---------------------
The full workflow was run on 365 real drone photos of a cotton field in
Larissa, Greece (the public CoFly-WeedDB dataset):
* All photos landed in the right place on the satellite map.
* Weeds covered 4.4% of the field in the weed map. Experts' labels cover about
  4.3-4.4% of the same photos.
* The whole field was analysed in about 7.5 minutes on a laptop.

WHAT TO KNOW (HONEST LIMITS)
----------------------------
* The drone app still needs one tap: Potensic offers no way for outside
  software to launch the drone. The pilot presses Start in the Eve app.
  On Android, missions load automatically; on iPhone, waypoints are entered by
  hand from the coordinates the app provides.
* Colour camera only: the Atom 2 has no thermal or near-infrared camera.
  Red areas mean "go and look", not a diagnosis. Early water stress, early
  disease and exact nitrogen levels need specialised cameras.
* Weed detection is good, not perfect: it finds weed patches, not species. It
  was trained on cotton, so other crops need extra training data to reach the
  same accuracy.
* Real what3words addresses need a paid what3words API key. A free test mode
  uses sample addresses (Donnelly Field, Cambridge MA; and the Greek test
  field).
* Always fly within sight and follow local drone rules.

COMING NEXT (PLANNED, NOT YET BUILT)
------------------------------------
* Irrigation check: map wet and dry soil around drip lines, spot dry gaps and
  waterlogged spots, and compare with recent rainfall.
* Changes over time: compare repeat flights to see which areas get worse.
* Deep-learning weed model: a larger neural network for higher accuracy.

2.1 SYSTEM OVERVIEW
-------------------
Layer             Technology / location
----------------  -----------------------------------------------------------
Desktop app       Electron 33 (upgraded CoFly-GUI), Leaflet maps, jQuery UI
Analysis engine   Node.js modules in js/analysis/; image handling with sharp
                  (libvips); no Python or GPU needed at run time
Drone link        js/drones/: Potensic Eve mission files, Android push via adb
what3words        js/w3w/: REST API v3 client, waypoint conversion, test mode
Phone web page    Express server in server/ (password protected, rate limited)
Command line      bin/w3w-fly.js
Model training    Python + scikit-learn, offline only (tools/)
Tests             61 automated tests (node --test), including real-data
                  regression tests

Every analysis step reads and writes files in the project folder
(projects/<name>/...), so steps can be rerun and results reload when a project
is reopened.

End-to-end flow:
  4 what3words corners -> field polygon -> coverage path -> Eve mission
  -> flight -> SD-card photos -> import (EXIF)
  -> 3D matching (structure from motion) -> orthomosaic -> 30 m tiles
     (or: per-photo analysis, photos placed by GPS)
  -> crop health + weeds + problem areas + field summary -> field maps
  -> close-up mission over problem areas -> close-up weed analysis

2.2 WHAT3WORDS
--------------
Files: js/w3w/what3words.js, js/w3w/waypoints.js, js/field_corners.js

* Lookup: GET https://api.what3words.com/v3/convert-to-coordinates
  (key sent as the X-Api-Key header). Returns the centre coordinates and the
  3 m square.
* Input checks: a Unicode-aware pattern for "word.word.word", with optional
  "///" in front.
* Repeat lookups are cached in memory.
* API errors (BadWords, InvalidKey, quota 402, network) become plain-language
  messages.
* Test mode: used automatically when no API key is set. Sample fixtures live in
  test/fixtures/w3w/. These are made-up addresses at real places: the four
  Donnelly Field corners (from the OpenStreetMap park boundary), four Larissa
  cotton-field corners, plus filled.count.soap (a real example address from the
  what3words documentation).
* Field corners: the four results are sorted by angle around their centre, so
  the outline never crosses itself. Area is computed with
  @mapbox/geojson-area; fields under 100 m2 are rejected.
* Waypoint conversion for iPhone (Eve does not accept what3words):
  - up to 30 addresses become numbered waypoints;
  - formats: decimal to 6 decimal places (~0.1 m), degrees-minutes-seconds,
    Apple Maps and Google Maps links;
  - optional clockwise loop order for field corners;
  - duplicate and invalid addresses are reported separately, without stopping
    the rest.

2.3 FLIGHT PLANNING AND THE POTENSIC ATOM 2
-------------------------------------------
Files: js/analysis/path_planner.js, js/drones/potensic_atom2.js,
       js/drones/adb_transfer.js

Coverage path:
* Projection: the field is converted to a local east/north metre grid
  (equirectangular, around the field centre), then rotated to the chosen
  flight direction.
* Lines: horizontal scan lines are intersected with the polygon (even-odd
  rule); no-fly zone intervals are subtracted; lines alternate direction
  (serpentine).
* Photo footprint: width = 2 x height x tan(horizontal FOV / 2).
* Spacing:
  - between lines = footprint width x (1 - 0.65 side overlap)
  - between photos = footprint length x (1 - 0.75 front overlap)
  Photo waypoints are placed along each line, because the Atom 2 only takes
  mission photos at waypoints.
* Outputs: waypoints, line count, flight time (distance / speed + hover time)
  and area.
* Close-up flights: problem points are ordered by nearest neighbour, flown at
  6 m by default.
* Fixes compared with CoFly's planner: CoFly's projection swapped latitude and
  longitude, which distorted spacing and failed beyond about +/-85 degrees
  longitude.

Eve mission format (unofficial; reverse-engineered by the HOT Drone Tasking
Manager team):
* Files: global.json (finishAction, lostAction, speed, ...) and <id>.json,
  holding "[waypoints];[pois]".
* Each waypoint: lat, lng, height, hoverTime, gimbalPitch, yaw, action PHOTO.
* Safety checks: height 2-120 m, gimbal -90..+20 degrees, speed up to 15 m/s.
  Missions are split at 99 points.
* Android: adb lists existing missions in
  /sdcard/Android/data/com.ipotensic.atom/files/Waypoint/, overwrites one with
  `adb push`, then force-stops Eve so it reloads.
  (Eve deletes mission folders it did not create, so a placeholder mission
  made in Eve is overwritten.) adb is found in common install locations.
* iPhone: app files cannot be written from outside, so the app outputs
  coordinates and an "enter in Eve" step list instead.

2.4 PHOTO IMPORT
----------------
File: js/analysis/photos.js

* EXIF read with piexifjs: GPS (degrees/minutes/seconds to decimal), capture
  time, camera make and model.
* Image size: read from the JPEG frame header, without decoding the image.
* Camera field of view by model:
  - DJI FC6310 (Phantom 4 Pro): 73.7 degrees
  - Potensic Atom 2: about 80 degrees (ASSUMED; Potensic doesn't publish it;
    can be overridden)
* Ground footprint comes from the flight height entered (EXIF altitude is
  above sea level, not above the field).
* Photo direction (heading):
  - "north": missions from this app (default);
  - "travel" and "travel-reverse": bearing between neighbouring photo
    positions, for photos from other apps.
  The default was confirmed on CoFly's DJI photos: crop rows only line up
  across neighbouring photos with a fixed heading.
* Photos without GPS are matched in order to the planned waypoints.
 
2.5 ORTHOMOSAIC / FIELD MAP STITCHING
-------------------------------------
Two methods. Step 4 offers "Automatic" (default: B when Docker is running and
the scan has at least 5 photos, otherwise A, with a note saying why),
"3D matching only" (B) and "GPS placement only" (A). Both produce the same
analysis.json and six map layers, so later steps work the same way.

A. GPS-based mosaic (js/analysis/mosaic.js)
   * Each footprint corner is rotated by heading into a local east/north
     frame. The union of all footprints defines the canvas.
   * Canvas: native resolution, capped at 5000 px on the long side.
   * Each tile: sharp resizes it, rotates it with a transparent background
     and composites it onto the canvas, in batches of 60.
   * The canvas edges become latitude/longitude bounds for a Leaflet image
     overlay.
   * The same layout renders every layer: photos, the four index maps, and
     weeds. Layers therefore line up exactly.
   * Limits:
     - not orthorectified: assumes flat ground and a straight-down camera;
     - no feature matching: neighbouring photos can be offset by the GPS
       error (typically 1-3 m).

B. Structure from motion (js/analysis/stitching.js, js/analysis/ortho_analysis.js)
   * Why: photos straight off the drone have 1-3 m GPS error, slight camera
     tilt, and the ground is not flat. OpenDroneMap finds features in every
     photo, matches them between overlapping photos, solves every camera's
     position and angle with a 3D point cloud (bundle adjustment), builds
     the surface and projects the photos onto it: one seamless orthomosaic.
   * Run: docker run opendronemap/odm:3.6.2 --fast-orthophoto --skip-3dmodel
     --skip-report --feature-quality medium --orthophoto-resolution 2
     (capped by ODM at the photos' own ground resolution). Photos whose
     position came from the flight plan are passed in geo.txt (--geo).
   * Re-projection and bounds: GDAL Python bindings inside the same container
     warp to EPSG:4326 with square ground pixels (the image has no gdalinfo
     on its PATH).
   * Analysis: shared colour ranges from a 1500 px copy; the map is cut into
     ~30 m tiles at full resolution and each tile runs through the same code
     as a photo (indices, problem areas, weed windows, 4 m summary areas).
     Transparent no-data pixels are ignored everywhere; summary areas less
     than half covered are dropped; tiles under 5% covered are skipped;
     field-wide numbers are weighted by tile coverage.
   * Memory: if Docker runs out of memory (exit 137), the reconstruction is
     kept and texturing is retried once at 4 cm/px (--rerun-from
     odm_meshing), with a note on raising Docker's memory.
   * Results are kept in scans/<id>/sfm/ and reused unless photos change;
     ODM's working files (~1 GB per scan) are deleted, orthophotos kept.
   * No crop: typical green cover under 5% -> "No crop seen" instead of a
     crop-health score (it would only compare soil colours).
   * Tested: 40 overlapping photos of an Ohio farm (CC0 dataset): 26 photos
     in one reconstruction, seamless map at 2.2 cm/px matching satellite
     imagery, 50 tiles analysed; ODM ~6.5 min + analysis ~3.5 min on an
     8 GB MacBook (Docker 4 GB). The Greek CoFly-WeedDB photos fail feature
     matching (too little overlap), so Automatic uses GPS placement there.


2.6 VEGETATION HEALTH ESTIMATION
--------------------------------
File: js/analysis/indices.js

* Four RGB indices, computed per pixel as Float32 and clipped to [-1, 1]
  (formulas from CoFly's MIT-licensed Vegetation-Indices module):
    GLI   = (2G - R - B) / (2G + R + B)    Green Leaf Index
    VARI  = (G - R) / (G + R - B)          Visible Atmospherically Resistant Index
    NGRDI = (G - R) / (G + R)              Normalised Green-Red Difference
    NGBDI = (G - B) / (G + B)              Normalised Green-Blue Difference
  (a small constant is added to each denominator)
* Display range: CoFly's rule.
  - A 100-bin histogram over [-1, 1]; keep the bins holding more than 5% of
    valid pixels.
  - Fallback: the 2nd-98th percentile.
  - For a field scan, one shared range is computed from a sub-sample of every
    photo, so colours are comparable across the field.
* Colour map: matplotlib's RdYlGn, red = low (stressed or bare), green = high
  (healthy). Transparent where there is no data.
* Outputs: a colour PNG per photo and per index; mean and low-value share per
  photo and per field.
* Limit: RGB only. These indices approximate canopy vigour; they are not
  NDVI.

2.7 PROBLEM AREA DETECTION
--------------------------
File: js/analysis/problem_areas.js

Steps:
1. Smooth over about 1.5 m with a box blur that ignores missing pixels. It
   uses running sums, so its cost does not depend on the blur size. This
   stops bare soil between crop rows from being reported.
2. Threshold: a pixel is flagged when its value is below
   min(lowest 25% of the display range, field median - 0.05).
   The median test stops sensor noise in an evenly healthy field from being
   flagged.
3. Clean-up: 5x5 binary closing, then opening. Pixels touching diagonally
   count as connected (8-connected flood-fill labelling).
4. Keep patches from 1 m2 up to 60% of the photo.
5. Merge patches within 4 m of each other (single-linkage), since one close-up
   photo covers them. Centres are weighted by patch area. This replaces
   CoFly's k-means grouping into 2-10 clusters.
6. Convert each centre from photo pixels to latitude/longitude, including the
   photo's rotation. Merge across photos. Output: up to 20 points per index,
   also written in CoFly's problematic_areas_points.json format.

Comparison with CoFly's original Python code. Their find_areas and
find_Lat_Lon were run unmodified on synthetic 50 x 37.5 m maps at 5 cm/px,
with crop rows every 0.9 m and noise. A patch counts as found if a point lands
within 1.5 m of its centre.

  Test case                 CoFly original              This project
  ------------------------  --------------------------  ----------------------
  two patches               0/2 found, 3 false alarms   2/2 found, 0 false
  two patches, no rows      crash                       2/2 found, 0 false
  two patches + map border  0/2 found, 4 false alarms   2/2 found, 0 false
  healthy field             2 false alarms              0 false alarms
  one patch                 0/1 found, 2 false alarms   1/1 found, 0 false
  patches + dark spot       crash                       2/2 found, 0 false
  twelve patches            2/12 found, 6 false alarms  12/12 found, 0 false
  worst map-position error  93 m (rows/cols swapped)    0 m

  The harness to reproduce this is in tools/compare_problem_areas/.

2.8 WEED DETECTION
------------------
Files: js/analysis/weed_model.js, js/analysis/photo_analysis.js,
       tools/train_weed_model.py, js/analysis/models/weed_gbt.json

Is it deep learning? NO.
CoFly's Weed-Detection code (a U-Net with an EfficientNet-B1 encoder) is
public, but its trained weights file (weights0300.hdf5) has never been
published anywhere, so that model cannot run. This project uses a trained
gradient-boosted decision-tree model instead.

Data:
* CoFly-WeedDB (Krestenitis et al. 2022, CC-BY-4.0): drone photos of cotton,
  Larissa, Greece, taken by a DJI Phantom 4 Pro at about 5 m.
* 201 photos with expert labels (3 weed species, merged into "weed").

Cells:
* The image is resized to 1280x720 and split into 40 x 22 = 880 cells, each
  about 19 cm on the ground.
* A cell's label is "weed" if at least half its pixels are labelled weed.
* Training set: 176,880 cells.

Features (36 per cell, computed by the same JavaScript code during training
and in the app):
* 18 basic features:
  - mean and spread of brightness, Excess Green and saturation;
  - Excess Red;
  - brightness-gradient mean and spread (texture), and edge density;
  - average red, green and blue proportions (chromaticity);
  - share of green-plant pixels (Excess Green with an automatic Otsu
    threshold);
  - brightness and Excess Green relative to the image median;
  - share of dark and of bright pixels.
* 2 crop-row features:
  - the row direction is found by testing angles from 0 to 180 degrees in
    3-degree steps and choosing the one where green pixels form the sharpest
    stripes;
  - row spacing comes from the self-similarity (autocorrelation) of that
    stripe pattern;
  - features: each cell's distance to the nearest row (as a fraction of the
    spacing), and whether rows were found.
* 14 neighbourhood features: 7 of the basic features averaged over the 3x3
  and 7x7 surrounding cells.
* 2 difference features: a cell compared with its neighbourhood.

Model (two-stage "auto-context"):
* Stage 1: scikit-learn HistGradientBoostingClassifier (250 iterations,
  31 leaves, learning rate 0.06, minimum 40 samples per leaf, L2 = 1.0).
* Stage 2: the same classifier, given the 36 features plus stage-1
  probabilities around each cell (its own; means over 3x3, 7x7 and 15x15
  cells; maximum over 7x7).
  Stage 2 is trained on stage-1 predictions made without seeing the same
  photo (4-fold cross-validation grouped by photo), so it learns from
  realistic inputs.
* Output: probabilities averaged over 3x3 cells, then a decision threshold
  chosen by cross-validation on training photos only (0.25 for the shipped
  model).
* Export: both tree ensembles are saved as JSON (0.9 MB) and run in plain
  JavaScript. The exported model reproduces scikit-learn's probabilities
  exactly (difference 0.0).

How the app applies it:
* Each photo is cut into windows about 7.5 m wide (the training scale). Each
  window is resized to 1280x720, run through the model, and the results are
  joined back into one weed map per photo.
* Photos coarser than 2 cm per pixel trigger a "fly lower" warning.
* Outputs: a transparent purple overlay, the photo with the overlay, weed
  cover %, and weed patch locations.

Accuracy on CoFly's three published test splits (weed IoU, i.e. overlap with
the experts' weed outlines, % / background IoU % / mean IoU %):

  Split   CoFly U-Net (reported,      This model         Earlier "green
          weights unpublished)        (in the app)       between rows" rule
  -----   -------------------------   ----------------   ------------------
  1       44.9 / 95.4 / 70.2          34.5 / 94.4 / 64.4  0.6 / 91.7 / 46.2
  2       43.5 / 95.8 / 69.7          30.8 / 94.3 / 62.6  0.6 / 87.7 / 44.1
  3       44.0 / 96.6 / 70.3          35.9 / 95.3 / 65.6  0.5 / 88.7 / 44.6

CoFly's model scores about 10 points higher on weed IoU, but it can't be run.
This model is the most accurate one that actually runs.

Upgrade path: train a U-Net on CoFly-WeedDB on a GPU, export it to ONNX, and
run it in the app with onnxruntime-node. That would add a real deep-learning
model without needing Python at run time.

2.9 FIELD HEALTH SUMMARY
------------------------
File: js/analysis/field_summary.js

Two headline numbers so the farmer doesn't have to read six maps.

Crop health score (0-100):
* Each photo is split into areas of about 4 x 4 m on the ground. That is wide
  enough to span several crop rows, so row spacing isn't mistaken for gaps.
* For each area:
  - green canopy cover, using the Canopeo rule (Patrignani & Ochsner 2015):
    R/G < 0.95, B/G < 0.95 and 2G - R - B > 20. These are fixed thresholds,
    so every photo is judged the same way. The per-photo Otsu threshold was
    tried first but split dark-green plants in contrasty photos;
  - plant greenness: GLI averaged over canopy pixels only;
  - the share of the area inside stressed/bare patches (section 2.7).
* An area "needs a look" if:
  - thin crop: cover < 70% of the field's median cover; or
  - pale crop: plant GLI more than 0.03 below the field's median; or
  - patchy: 25% or more of the area is in stressed/bare patches.
* Score = 100 x (share of areas that do not need a look).
  Ratings: 85-100 Good, 65-84 Fair, below 65 Poor.
* The flagged areas are saved with their map corners. "Show where to look"
  draws them on the map with the reason.
* Limit: the score compares each part of the field with the rest of the same
  field, which keeps it consistent across cameras and light conditions. It
  cannot detect a field that is evenly stressed; compare scans over time for
  that.

Weeds (%):
* The share of the photographed field covered by likely weed patches (weed
  model, section 2.8), averaged over all photos.
* Ratings: under 2% Low, 2-10% Moderate, over 10% High.

Result on the Greek cotton field (365 photos, 730 areas):
* Crop health 96/100 (Good). Areas needing a look: 3% thin crop, 1.9% pale
  crop, 0.4% bare/stressed patches.
* Weeds 4.4% (Moderate).
* Checked by eye: flagged areas are sparse or bare spots (field edges, missing
  plants); the typical areas are full crop rows.
* Older analyses get the summary added on demand (about 0.2 s per photo)
  without re-running the full analysis.

2.10 FIELD ANALYSIS PIPELINE
----------------------------
File: js/analysis/pipeline.js

* planScan: field polygon + no-fly zones -> coverage waypoints -> Eve
  missions. Also writes CoFly's calculated_path.json, so the original map
  drawing still works.
* importScan: copies the photos to scans/<id>/images and records photo
  height and heading.
* analyzeScan (method auto / sfm / gps; sfm = section 2.5 B):
  - pass 1: index values at 300 px -> one shared colour range per index;
  - pass 2, per photo (about 1.2 s on Apple Silicon): index maps, problem
    areas and weed windows;
  - then the 6 stitched layers (photos, GLI, VARI, NGRDI, NGBDI, weeds), the
    field health summary (section 2.9) and the field summary (analysis.json).
* planHotpoints: problem points -> close-up mission (6 m, nearest-neighbour
  route).
* importCloseups: weed analysis of the close-up photos.
* analyzeSinglePhoto: crop health and weeds for one what3words photo, with a
  warning if the photo's GPS is far from the square.

Measured on the Greek test field (365 photos, 5 m, DJI Phantom 4 Pro):
* Import: under 1 s. Analysis: about 6.7 min. Stitching: about 40 s.
* Weed cover: 4.4% (experts' labels: about 4.3-4.4%).
* GLI problem patches: 0.2% of the area (soil between rows correctly not
  flagged).

2.12 IRRIGATION CHECKS (FEASIBILITY - NOT YET IMPLEMENTED)
----------------------------------------------------------
The best signal for crop water stress is thermal imaging (canopy temperature),
which the Atom 2 lacks. RGB-based checks that fit the current pipeline:

1. Wet/dry soil map:
   - remove plants with the existing Excess Green mask;
   - classify the remaining soil pixels by brightness and colour (wet soil is
     darker);
   - measure how far water spreads from each drip line (drip lines are
     visible in the Greek photos);
   - flag dry gaps (possible blocked drippers) and dark standing-water areas
     (over-watering or poor drainage).
2. Irrigation-aligned stress: test whether low-index strips run parallel to
   the drip lines or line up with irrigation zones, using the existing
   row-direction code.
3. Change over time: compare index maps from repeat scans.
4. Rainfall context: recent rainfall for the field from a free weather API
   (e.g. Open-Meteo; CHIRPS for research use). This separates irrigation
   faults from weather.
5. Optional satellite layer: Sentinel-1 or Sentinel-2 irrigation and moisture
   products for whole-farm context.

Cannot be done with RGB alone: measuring soil moisture, or detecting water
stress before it is visible. These need thermal cameras or soil sensors.

  - export_weed_features.js, train_weed_model.py, eval_weed_model.js;
  - compare_problem_areas/: the comparison with CoFly's code.

Not yet tested: a real Atom 2 flight, a real phone and a live what3words key.
